Serverless Postgres is PostgreSQL delivered so that you never provision or manage a database server: the platform allocates compute on demand, scales it with load, and bills you for usage rather than for an instance size you picked up front. The term covers genuinely different architectures, and the differences that matter in practice are how each provider handles idle time (scale-to-zero and cold starts) and what unit it bills (compute-hours, operations, or instances). This guide explains the moving parts and profiles the five providers you will meet on most shortlists: [Prisma Postgres](https://www.prisma.io/docs/postgres), Neon, Supabase, Aurora Serverless v2, and PlanetScale. It then maps the rest of the field, from Xata and Nile to DigitalOcean, Cloud SQL, and Heroku, onto the same framework, and gives you a workload-based way to choose. The comparison scopes deliberately to serverless behavior and pricing structure; a production decision should also weigh extensions, read replicas, backup and restore, regions, and compliance, which are not covered here. Every price and behavior cited was checked against the provider's own published pages in July 2026, and claims that are a vendor's own (including Prisma's) are labeled as such.

## What does "serverless" mean for a database?

A serverless database separates compute from storage, scales compute automatically with load, and charges for what you use instead of what you provision. For Postgres specifically, providers implement this in three different ways: metered compute that suspends when idle, a managed always-on service metered per query, or dedicated instances that autoscale but never suspend.

That definition hides real disagreement. Neon and Aurora treat "serverless" as elastic compute you rent by the hour or second. Prisma Postgres treats it as a database you interact with purely through queries, with the compute invisible. Supabase and PlanetScale sell instance-shaped databases with managed operations, and only parts of their offerings behave in a serverless way. When you compare providers, compare behaviors, not the label.

### The building blocks

- **Compute and storage separation.** Your data lives in a storage layer independent of the Postgres process serving queries. This is what makes everything else possible: compute can stop, restart, resize, or fork without touching the data.
- **Autoscaling.** Compute grows and shrinks with load. Neon sizes compute in CUs (1 CU is 1 vCPU and 4 GB RAM); Aurora sizes it in ACUs (1 ACU is roughly 2 GB RAM, scaling in half-ACU increments) billed per second.
- **Scale-to-zero.** Compute suspends entirely after an idle period, so an unused database costs storage only. Neon suspends after 5 minutes by default; Aurora pauses after a configurable interval (5 minutes minimum) when you set minimum capacity to 0 ACUs; Supabase pauses only free-tier projects, after 1 week of inactivity.
- **Cold starts.** The other side of scale-to-zero: the first query after a suspension has to wait for compute to wake. Concrete numbers are below; on the platforms that suspend, they range from a few hundred milliseconds to about 30 seconds. Several configurations avoid them entirely: PlanetScale on every tier, Supabase on paid plans, Neon with auto-suspend disabled, and Prisma Postgres by design.
- **Branching.** Because storage is copy-on-write in several of these architectures, you can fork a database like a Git branch: an isolated copy for a preview deployment or a migration test that shares unchanged data with its parent. Neon includes 10 branches per project free; Supabase bills branches at $0.01344 per hour; PlanetScale runs every branch as its own billed cluster. Prisma Postgres has no database branching today; its closest workflow is creating separate databases, which its plans allow in large numbers (50 free, 1,000 on paid).
- **Connection pooling.** Postgres handles a limited number of direct connections, and serverless functions multiply connections fast. Prisma Postgres, Neon, and Supabase build a pooler into the platform; on Aurora the standard answer is RDS Proxy, a separately billed AWS service with a consequential caveat: it holds connections open, which prevents Aurora's auto-pause from ever engaging.

## The three pricing models

Pricing structure, more than any feature, determines which provider fits a workload. The category has settled into three models:

| Model | Who uses it | You pay for | Favors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compute-time | Neon, Aurora Serverless v2 | Hours (or seconds) of compute, sized to load, plus storage | Bursty or idle-heavy workloads that can scale to zero |
| Operations | Prisma Postgres | Each query your app makes, regardless of compute time, plus storage | Steady predictability per request; apps that want a bill proportional to traffic |
| Instances | PlanetScale, Supabase | A dedicated cluster or instance size, always on, prorated by time | Sustained high throughput where dedicated capacity beats metering |

The practical consequences:

- **Compute-time billing** rewards idleness. A database that sleeps 20 hours a day costs a fraction of an always-on one. It also means a traffic spike is billed at the size of the compute that served it, and a workload that never idles loses the model's main advantage.
- **Operation billing** makes the variable part of the bill track traffic instead of time; the plans themselves are flat monthly tiers with included allowances, so the floor is the plan price ($10/month at entry) rather than zero. There is no idle-versus-active math to do. The flip side is that the query count is the bill: query-heavy patterns (N+1 queries, chatty polling) cost more, and at sustained high volume the math flips. A worked example with this guide's own numbers: an app doing 30M queries a month costs about $89 on the $49 operations plan (10M included, 20M overage at $0.002 per 1,000), while a dedicated always-on 1-CU compute on Neon runs about $77/month ($0.106 × 730 hours) with no per-query component at all. Where your traffic sits relative to that crossover should drive the choice.
- **Instance billing** is the most predictable of the three: a fixed monthly price per branch or project. You give up the pay-nothing-when-idle property entirely, which is why PlanetScale positions itself for production workloads that are never idle.

### Prisma Postgres

[Prisma Postgres](https://www.prisma.io/docs/postgres) is a managed Postgres platform built on unikernels running in microVMs on bare metal, an architecture designed so databases are always ready without cold starts and without billing for idle compute (disclosure: Prisma publishes this guide, and the no-cold-start claim is ours; the architecture is documented but not independently benchmarked). You pay per operation, defined on the [pricing page](https://www.prisma.io/pricing) as "each time you interact with your database, no matter the compute time. We count the Prisma ORM queries you make, not the SQL statements you run." If your stack queries mostly outside Prisma ORM, check the pricing docs for how those are counted before doing the math. The free tier includes 100,000 operations per month, 500 MB of storage, and 50 databases with no credit card (allowances are account-wide, not per database); paid plans run from $10/month (1M operations, 10 GB included, then $0.008 per 1,000 operations) to $129/month (50M operations, overage $0.001 per 1,000), with spend limits and egress included on all plans. Account-wide metering suits fleets of small databases (per-user, per-agent, per-preview) especially well. The honest trade-offs: there is no database branching today (separate databases are the substitute workflow), the platform lacks the operational track record of Aurora or Supabase, and at sustained high query volume the per-operation math can exceed always-on billing; see the worked example above. Local development uses a local Prisma Postgres instance (powered by PGlite) via `npx prisma dev`, and `npx create-db` provisions a temporary cloud database in seconds without an account (claim it to keep it beyond 24 hours).

### Neon

Neon popularized serverless Postgres and remains the reference implementation of the compute-time model: compute sized in CUs, metered per hour, suspending after 5 minutes idle. Its [pricing](https://neon.com/pricing) is pay-as-you-go with no monthly minimum: $0.106/CU-hour on Launch, $0.222/CU-hour on Scale, storage at $0.35/GB-month, and a free tier that is genuinely per project: each of up to 100 projects gets its own 100 CU-hours and 0.5 GB per month. Neon's own docs are candid that activating a compute from idle "typically takes a few hundred milliseconds," and paid plans can disable auto-suspend to trade idle savings for zero cold starts. In May 2025 Databricks agreed to acquire Neon (reported at roughly $1 billion), committing to continued investment in the platform; Neon's own docs state it "continues as a standalone serverless Postgres platform," positioned alongside Databricks Lakebase, and as of July 2026 that is how it operates. Databricks's acquisition blog post also carried a striking data point about where the category is heading: by Neon's telemetry, the share of its databases created by AI agents rather than humans had grown from about 30% around Neon's GA to over 80% as of the May 2025 announcement.

### Supabase

Supabase is a backend platform (auth, storage, realtime, edge functions) with Postgres at its center, rather than a pure database service, and it does not primarily market itself as serverless Postgres. Its Postgres is instance-shaped: [plans](https://supabase.com/pricing) include $10/month of compute credits covering one Micro instance (2-core ARM, 1 GB RAM), with larger dedicated instances from $15 to $3,730/month billed hourly. The free tier allows 2 projects with 500 MB database storage each, and pauses projects after 1 week of inactivity; paid projects never pause, so they have no cold starts. Branching is billed at $0.01344 per branch-hour. Choose Supabase when you want the bundled backend; measured against the serverless behaviors defined above (autoscaling, scale-to-zero, usage billing), it exhibits the fewest of the five, which matters only if serverless behavior is what you came for.

### Aurora Serverless v2

Aurora Serverless v2 is AWS's elastic Postgres (and MySQL), billing capacity in ACU-seconds within a range you configure. Since late 2024 it supports true scale-to-zero: set minimum capacity to 0 ACUs and instances auto-pause after a configurable idle interval (5 minutes minimum), with no compute charge while paused. AWS's [own documentation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/aurora-serverless-v2-auto-pause.html) is precise about the trade-off: "the typical time to resume might be approximately 15 seconds," rising to 30 seconds or more if the instance was paused over 24 hours, and it recommends client timeouts above 15 seconds. Several common configurations silently defeat auto-pause: an attached RDS Proxy, logical replication, or being the writer in a global database all keep instances awake. Pricing is per ACU-second and varies by region and configuration; use the [AWS pricing page](https://aws.amazon.com/rds/aurora/pricing/) to model your workload. Aurora fits teams already deep in AWS who need its ecosystem (IAM, VPC peering, global databases) more than they need simplicity.

### PlanetScale Postgres

PlanetScale, long a MySQL/Vitess platform, entered Postgres in 2025: general availability on September 22, 2025, with plans from $5/month, and its Metal tier (locally attached NVMe, storage included) generally available for Postgres on December 15, 2025 at a new $50/month entry point, down from $589. PlanetScale takes the opposite position from Neon on the core question: there is no scale-to-zero at all. Every branch runs on its own dedicated, always-on cluster, [billed](https://planetscale.com/docs/postgres/pricing) by instance size prorated to the millisecond, from $5/month (single node) and $15/month (highly available) up to five figures for the largest Metal instances. There is no free tier on its current pricing page. In March 2026, the Drizzle ORM team joined PlanetScale, with Drizzle stated to remain an independent open source project. PlanetScale fits sustained production workloads where predictable dedicated performance justifies paying for every hour.

## The rest of the field

The five profiles above cover the main shapes serverless Postgres takes. Most of the rest of the managed-Postgres market is not serverless at all, and knowing which is which is half the value of the category label. All checked against vendor pages in July 2026.

**Serverless-adjacent players:**

- **Xata** relaunched in 2025 as a Postgres platform built around copy-on-write branching ("one Postgres, thousands of branches"), with idle branches hibernating at no compute cost and waking in about a second on its cloud. Billing is hourly per instance (from $0.012/hour) plus $0.28/GB-month storage; there is no permanent free cloud tier (new signups get $100 in 14-day credits), and Xata open-sourced its core under Apache 2.0 in 2026.
- **Nile** is serverless Postgres re-engineered for multi-tenant apps: every tenant is a virtual database inside one Postgres, billed in query tokens (free tier with 50M tokens and 1 GB; Pro from $15/month). Its architectural bet is multi-tenant compute that never pauses, so it claims scale-to-zero cost without cold starts.
- **Tembo**, previously a managed-Postgres platform, pivoted in May 2025 to running AI coding agents and no longer markets a Postgres product. Worth knowing if you shortlisted it from an older comparison.

**Managed instances, fully managed but not serverless.** DigitalOcean (from $15.15/month), Render (from $6/month, billed per second; free databases expire after 30 days), and Heroku Postgres (standard plans from $5/month; its newer Advanced tier decouples storage billing but still neither autoscales nor suspends) all sell instance sizes you pick and resize yourself: no compute autoscaling, no scale-to-zero, and none of the three uses the word serverless for these products. Railway is the interesting edge case: it bills actual usage per minute ($10/GB RAM, $20/vCPU per month) rather than a fixed size, and has an opt-in sleep feature for idle services, though its docs note that open database connections prevent sleeping and don't state whether volume-backed Postgres services can sleep at all.

**The other hyperscaler options.** Aurora Serverless v2 (profiled above) is the only hyperscaler Postgres that suspends automatically. Google Cloud SQL bills fixed machine types per second and supports manual stop only; Google AlloyDB has fixed primary compute and no suspend (read pools can autoscale horizontally, in preview; a 30-day free trial cluster, no permanent free tier); Azure Database for PostgreSQL Flexible Server is fixed SKUs from about $12/month, with a manual stop that Azure automatically reverses after 7 days. None of the three is branded serverless by its own vendor.

The test that sorts the field: if you pick an instance size and the vendor never suspends it for you, that is managed Postgres, which may be exactly what you need. Applied honestly, the test also grades the five profiled providers unevenly: Prisma Postgres, Neon, and Aurora Serverless v2 pass it outright, while PlanetScale and paid Supabase are instance-shaped and earn their profiles by being on the same shortlists, not by passing every serverless test. Xata and Nile pass it at smaller scale.

## Cold starts: the dividing line

Pricing structure decides what you pay; cold start behavior decides what your users feel. If your application has idle periods and latency-sensitive users, it is the operational difference you will notice first. Where a provider suspends compute, the number below is from its own documentation; where a provider has no cold starts, the reason is stated instead:

- **Neon:** activating a compute from idle "typically takes a few hundred milliseconds," per Neon's connection-latency documentation. Paid plans can disable auto-suspend entirely.
- **Aurora Serverless v2:** resume from 0 ACUs takes "approximately 15 seconds" typically, and 30 seconds or more after a pause longer than 24 hours, per AWS's auto-pause documentation.
- **Supabase:** paid projects never pause, so no cold starts; free-tier projects pause after a week of inactivity and require a restore.
- **PlanetScale:** no cold starts because nothing ever sleeps; you pay for the always-on instance.
- **Prisma Postgres:** no cold starts, by architecture rather than by paying for idle time: unikernel microVMs are designed so there is no suspended state to resume from. This is our own claim about our own product, published in our [architecture write-up](https://www.prisma.io/blog/prisma-postgres-the-future-of-serverless-databases) and not independently benchmarked; hold it to the same skepticism as any vendor claim.

The structural point: on compute-time platforms, cold starts are the price of the savings, and eliminating them there means paying for idle time again.

Match the provider to the shape of your workload:

- **Side project, prototype, or agent-generated app:** you want a generous free tier and zero setup. All three free tiers here work: Prisma Postgres (100k operations, 50 databases, no card), Neon (100 CU-hours across 100 projects), and Supabase (2 projects, if week-idle pausing is acceptable).
- **Spiky or scheduled workloads (cron jobs, internal tools, dev/test fleets):** compute-time billing with scale-to-zero was built for this. Neon, or Aurora if you're already on AWS and can tolerate ~15-second resumes.
- **User-facing apps with idle periods but latency-sensitive users:** this is where cold starts hurt. Four ways out, at different price shapes: operation-based billing (Prisma Postgres keeps the database ready and bills the queries), Neon with auto-suspend disabled, or a small always-on instance on PlanetScale ($5) or paid Supabase ($25). Which is cheapest depends on query volume and how small an instance your load fits; the worked example above shows how to compare.
- **Steady, high-throughput production:** always-on dedicated capacity wins on price-per-query at sustained load. PlanetScale (from $5 single node, $50 Metal), or Aurora sized for the load.
- **You want the whole backend, not just the database:** Supabase, and accept the instance-shaped economics.
- **Many small databases (per-tenant, per-user, per-AI-agent):** account-level metering across a fleet matters more than per-database pricing. Prisma Postgres meters operations across all databases on one plan; Neon's per-project free allowance also stretches far here. This pattern is growing: Neon's telemetry from May 2025 showed the share of databases created on its platform by AI agents rather than humans had passed 80%.

## Summary

Serverless Postgres in 2026 is three pricing models wearing one name. Compute-time platforms (Neon, Aurora) turn idle time into savings and charge for it in cold starts. Instance platforms (PlanetScale, Supabase) turn predictability into a flat bill and give up idle savings. Operation-based billing (Prisma Postgres) prices the queries themselves, keeping databases always ready, with a flat plan floor and query volume as the number to watch. Pick by workload shape: how idle your database is, how sensitive your users are to a slow first query, and whether your bill should track time or traffic.

To try the operation-based model, create a [Prisma Postgres](https://www.prisma.io/docs/postgres) database in seconds with `npx create-db` (no account needed; the database is temporary until you claim it), or read how its [unikernel architecture](https://www.prisma.io/blog/prisma-postgres-the-future-of-serverless-databases) removes cold starts. For the cost side of this comparison in detail, Prisma Postgres and Neon pricing get a dedicated line-by-line breakdown in an upcoming companion post.
